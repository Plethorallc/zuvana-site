How exchange rate markups work when sending money abroad

Updated: October 2026

When you send money abroad, you probably look at the fee. But there is a second cost that is easy to miss and often larger: the exchange rate markup. Understanding how it works can help you keep more of your money — and help your family receive more. Here is a plain-English explanation.

What is an exchange rate?

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. If one U.S. dollar buys 18 Mexican pesos, the USD/MXN rate is 18. Exchange rates move constantly during the day, driven by trading between banks and other large financial institutions around the world.

The mid-market rate: the benchmark

At any moment, currency markets have a price at which people are willing to buy and a price at which they are willing to sell. The midpoint between those two is called the mid-market rate (also called the interbank or reference rate). It is the rate you typically see when you search “USD to MXN” in a search engine or check a central bank reference such as the one published by Banco de México.

The mid-market rate is the fairest benchmark for comparing offers, because it doesn’t include anyone’s profit.

What is a markup?

Most consumers can’t trade at the mid-market rate directly. Instead, a bank or money transfer provider converts your money for you. Many providers give you a rate that is less favorable than mid-market and keep the difference. That difference is the exchange rate markup (sometimes called a spread or margin).

Here’s a hypothetical example to show the math:

  • Mid-market rate: 18.00 pesos per dollar
  • Rate a provider offers you: 17.64 pesos per dollar
  • Markup: 0.36 pesos per dollar, which is 2% of the mid-market rate

On a $500 transfer, the mid-market rate would deliver 9,000 pesos. At the provider’s rate, your recipient gets 8,820 pesos. That’s 180 pesos less — about $10 — even if the provider advertises “no fee.”

Why markups are easy to miss

  • They aren’t labeled as a fee. A receipt may show “Fee: $0” while the cost is built into the rate.
  • Rates change all day. Without checking the mid-market rate at that moment, it’s hard to know how far off an offer is.
  • Small differences add up. A few cents per dollar sounds trivial, but it scales with every dollar you send and every month you send it.
  • Promotions can shift costs. A “free transfer” may use a worse rate, so the total cost isn’t actually lower.

How to calculate the markup yourself

  1. Look up the mid-market rate right before you send.
  2. Note the rate the provider offers.
  3. Use this formula: markup % = (mid-market rate − offered rate) ÷ mid-market rate × 100.
  4. Add the markup percentage to the fee as a percentage of your transfer. That sum is your true cost.

For example, if the fee is $4 on a $200 transfer (2%) and the markup is 1.5%, your true cost is about 3.5% of what you send — around $7.

The simplest test: amount received

If formulas aren’t your thing, there’s a shortcut. Ask each provider: “If I pay this many dollars, exactly how much will arrive in the other currency?” That one number already includes fees and the markup. Compare providers on the same amount, same payment method, and at the same time of day.

In the United States, federal rules for consumer remittance transfers, overseen by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), generally require providers to disclose the exchange rate, fees, and the amount to be received before you pay. That disclosure is your best tool for spotting a markup.

Other places conversions hide

  • Card payments abroad. When paying by card in another country, you may be offered to be charged in your home currency. This “dynamic currency conversion” often comes with a poor rate. Choosing the local currency is usually cheaper.
  • Airport and hotel exchanges. Convenience locations often have wide spreads.
  • Bank wires. Some banks apply their own rate to international wires in addition to a wire fee.

Is a markup ever fair?

Providers have real costs: moving money across borders, complying with regulations, preventing fraud, and paying staff. Charging for that is reasonable. The problem isn’t that providers charge — it’s when the charge is hard to see. A transparent model shows you exactly what you pay, whether as a clear fee, a clear markup, or both. That way you can make a fair comparison.

Key takeaways

  • The mid-market rate is the benchmark; most offers are below it.
  • The gap is a markup — a real cost even when the fee says $0.
  • Compare the amount received, not the advertised fee.
  • Express all costs as a percentage of what you send to see the true price.
